# Dominican Republic: Verified Statistics on Poverty & Education

Every figure below is sourced to a primary institutional document (World Bank, OECD,
UNICEF, ILO/US DOL). Each entry lists the **exact figure**, the **data year**, and the
**source document** so any claim on the site can be defended if challenged.

Confidence labels:
- **[A] Primary-verified** — pulled directly from the source document text.
- **[B] Institutional, secondary-read** — reputable institutional source, figure read via
  its published summary rather than the underlying table.
- **[C] Advocacy estimate** — credible but contested or not officially measured. Attribute
  explicitly ("according to X") if used at all.

---

## 1. The learning crisis — the strongest material on this list

This is where the Dominican Republic is a genuine global outlier, and where the numbers are
both extreme and airtight.

### 1.1 Only 8% of 15-year-olds can do basic math **[A]**
> In the Dominican Republic, **8% of students attained at least Level 2 proficiency in
> mathematics**, significantly less than on average across OECD countries (**OECD average:
> 69%**).

Level 2 is the OECD's baseline — the level at which a student can recognise, without being
told how, that a simple situation can be represented mathematically (e.g. comparing the
total distance of two alternative routes). **92% of Dominican 15-year-olds cannot.**

- Year: PISA 2022
- Source: OECD, *PISA 2022 Results (Volume I and II) — Country Note: Dominican Republic*, p. 2

### 1.2 81% of children reach late primary age unable to read a simple text **[A]**
> **81 percent** of children in Dominican Republic at late primary age today are not
> proficient in reading, adjusted for out-of-school children.

Components of that figure: **79%** of students in school do not reach minimum reading
proficiency, and **7%** of primary-school-age children are not enrolled at all.

Benchmarks: this is **30 percentage points worse** than the Latin America & Caribbean
average and **51.8 percentage points worse** than the upper-middle-income country average.

- Year: Brief published Oct 2019; underlying assessment grade 6, 2013
- Source: World Bank, *Dominican Republic Learning Poverty Brief*, October 2019
- Caveat: this is the most quotable single number available, but it is the oldest. Date it
  explicitly on the site ("as of the World Bank's most recent learning poverty brief").

### 1.3 Most teenagers cannot read at a basic level either **[A]**
> In reading, **70% of girls and 81% of boys scored below Level 2**.

- Year: PISA 2022
- Source: OECD, *PISA 2022 Country Note: Dominican Republic*, p. 3

### 1.4 Roughly 130 points behind the OECD, in every subject **[B]**

| Subject | Dominican Republic | OECD average | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mathematics | 339 | 472 | −133 |
| Reading | 351 | 476 | −125 |
| Science | 360 | 485 | −125 |

Among the 81 education systems tested, the Dominican Republic's results are among the
lowest recorded.

- Year: PISA 2022
- Source: OECD Education GPS / *PISA 2022 Results (Volume I)*, Dominican Republic profile
- Note: avoid stating a precise world rank — published "rankings" mix subjects and
  subnational entries. "Among the lowest of the 81 systems tested" is defensible.

### 1.5 Almost no student reaches the top **[A]**
> Almost no students were top performers in mathematics (Level 5 or 6): **nearly 0%**,
> against an **OECD average of 9%**.

- Year: PISA 2022
- Source: OECD, *PISA 2022 Country Note: Dominican Republic*

### 1.6 A child born here will be only half as productive as they could be **[A]**
> A child born in the Dominican Republic today will be **50 percent as productive** when
> she grows up as she could be if she enjoyed complete education and full health.

And the reason is schooling quality, not schooling quantity:
> A child who starts school at age 4 can expect to complete **11.9 years of school** by her
> 18th birthday. Factoring in what children actually learn, expected years of school is
> only **6.6 years**.

**More than five years of schooling are lost to poor learning quality.** World average for
the productivity figure is 56%.

- Year: Human Capital Index 2020
- Source: World Bank, *Human Capital Index — Dominican Republic*, October 2020

### 1.7 It is not a money problem — and that is the point **[A]**
> Primary education expenditure per child of primary education age in Dominican Republic is
> **USD 2,540 (PPP)**, which is **3.9% above the average for the Latin America and
> Caribbean region** and **13.1% above the average for upper middle income countries**.

The country spends more per primary-age child than its region and its income group, and
still posts the region's worst learning results. This is the most persuasive framing
available: the gap is in teaching quality and instruction, not in budget.

- Year: 2017 expenditure data
- Source: World Bank, *Dominican Republic Learning Poverty Brief*, October 2019

### 1.8 Secondary school is where children disappear **[B]**
- Secondary school completion: **61%**
- Adolescents of lower and upper secondary age out of school: **~16%**
- Secondary students enrolled two or more years over-age: **24.1%** (a leading predictor of
  dropout)

- Year: 2024–2025
- Source: UNICEF Dominican Republic, *Annual Report 2025*

### 1.9 Third grade, lowest performance band **[B]**
**80.1%** of Dominican third-grade students scored at the lowest reading performance level
in the regional ERCE assessment, improving only marginally by sixth grade. Regionally,
about four in ten third- and sixth-graders were at the lowest level.

- Year: ERCE 2019 (UNESCO/LLECE)
- Source: UNESCO LLECE, *ERCE 2019 national results report — República Dominicana*
- Caveat: verify against the national report PDF before publishing; this one came through a
  secondary read.

## 2. Poverty and inequality — accurate numbers, handle with care

**Read this before using anything in this section.** Dominican income poverty is moderate
and falling quickly: official poverty dropped from 23% to 19% in a single year, and extreme
poverty is **2.4%**. Framing the country as one of extreme material destitution is not
supported by the current data and will be fact-checked against the World Bank's own brief.
The defensible poverty story is **who is poor** (children, rural households) and **what
poverty does to learning** — not headline destitution.

### 2.1 Children are the poorest age group in the country **[A]**
Poverty rate at the $8.30/day upper-middle-income line, 2024:

| Group | Poverty rate |
|---|---|
| **Children 0–14** | **25.2%** |
| Adults 15–64 | 10.8% |
| Adults 65+ | 6.6% |

A Dominican child is nearly **four times** as likely to be poor as a Dominican pensioner.
This is a strong, verified, non-obvious stat.

- Source: World Bank, *Poverty & Equity Brief — Dominican Republic*, October 2025, p. 4

### 2.2 Headline poverty figures **[A]**
- Official national poverty: **19%** in 2024, down from 23% in 2023
- Extreme poverty: **2.4%** in 2024, down from 3.2%
- Living on less than $8.30/day: **14.0%** (2024)
- Living on less than $3.00/day: **0.8%** (2024)
- Gini index: **39.0** (2024), up from 38.4 — inequality has risen every year since 2022

- Source: World Bank, *Poverty & Equity Brief — Dominican Republic*, October 2025

### 2.3 Household deprivation **[A]**
Share of the population living in a household where:
- No adult has completed primary education: **13.9%**
- At least one school-aged child is not enrolled in school: **3.8%**
- No access to limited-standard drinking water: **6.0%**
- No access to limited-standard sanitation: **4.5%**

- Year: 2024
- Source: World Bank, *Poverty & Equity Brief — Dominican Republic*, October 2025, p. 4

### 2.4 The rural gap **[A]**
- Piped water on premises: **76.6% rural** vs 87.8% urban
- Internet access: **32.2% of rural households** vs 45.9% urban
- Average labour income in the southern region is **82%** of that in the Ozama (Santo
  Domingo) region
- Rural poverty 15.0% vs urban 13.8%, and rural poverty is falling **less than half as
  fast** (−1.8pp vs −4.4pp in 2024)

- Year: 2024
- Source: World Bank, *Poverty & Equity Brief — Dominican Republic*, October 2025

### 2.5 Child poverty, UNICEF's measure **[B]**
- Multidimensional child poverty: **18%** (2024), down from 21% — but **26% in rural areas**
- Child monetary poverty: **31%** (2024), down from 36%
- Child extreme poverty: **4%**, down from 6%

- Source: UNICEF Dominican Republic, *Annual Report 2024 / 2025*

## 3. Girls, early unions and youth

### 3.1 More than a third of young women enter a union as children **[B]**
> More than **one third of young women aged between 18 and 22** get married or form an
> informal union before turning 18, and **one in five has already given birth** before
> reaching that age.

The Dominican Republic has among the highest rates of child marriage and early union in
Latin America and the Caribbean — the only world region where child marriage has not
declined in 25 years. Child marriage has been illegal since Law 1-21 (2021); the practice
continues.

- Source: World Bank blog, *In the Dominican Republic, child marriage is not only a moral
  issue, but an economic one as well*; UNICEF LAC
- Note: UNICEF's country profile reports **32% married before 18 / 9% before 15** on the
  standard "women aged 20–24" measure. Pick one measure and cite it precisely — the age
  bands differ between sources and mixing them is the most likely way to get caught out.

### 3.2 A quarter of young people are neither working nor studying **[B]**
- Youth not in education, employment or training (NEET): **25.7%** overall
- Young women: **30.8%**

- Year: 2023
- Source: World Bank World Development Indicators (SL.UEM.NEET.ZS)

### 3.3 Women are locked out of the labour market **[A]**
- Female labour force participation: **54%** vs **79%** for men (2024)
- Women's labour income: **81%** of men's average income
- The World Bank attributes part of this gap directly to the prevalence of teenage
  pregnancy and the lower educational attainment that follows it

- Source: World Bank, *Poverty & Equity Brief — Dominican Republic*, October 2025

### 3.4 Child labour **[B]**
- **6.3%** of children aged 5–14 are working
- **9.2%** of children aged 7–14 combine work and school
- In 2024 the country made **minimal advancement** in eliminating the worst forms of child
  labour

- Source: US Department of Labor, *2024 Findings on the Worst Forms of Child Labor —
  Dominican Republic*

## 4. Statelessness and documentation — use only with attribution **[C]**

Children of Haitian descent born in the Dominican Republic can be denied birth
registration, which blocks school enrolment and national exams.

- As many as **130,000** people are estimated to remain stateless following the 2013
  Constitutional Court ruling; **86%** of those affected were of Haitian descent
- A remediation programme registered **8,755 people out of 53,000 targeted**
- Bateyes (sugar-estate settlements) are home to an estimated **200,000+** residents with
  limited access to water, electricity, education and legal services

- Sources: Center for Migration Studies; Minority Rights Group; UNESCO, *Migration,
  displacement and education in Dominican Republic*
- Caveat: these are NGO and academic estimates, not official statistics, and the population
  totals vary widely between sources. Always write "according to the Center for Migration
  Studies…" rather than stating them flatly. The batey population range in circulation
  (200,000 to 1 million) is too wide to quote as a single number.

## 5. Stats to avoid

Four figures that look useful and will backfire:

1. **Child stunting / chronic malnutrition.** At **6.7%**, the Dominican Republic is *below*
   the Latin America & Caribbean average of 11.3%, and down from 21.2% in 1990. A hunger
   framing is not supported.
2. **Extreme poverty as a headline.** **2.4%** is low and falling. Quoting "extreme poverty
   in the Dominican Republic" invites an easy correction.
3. **Teenage pregnancy as "the worst in the region."** The adolescent fertility rate is
   **53.6 per 1,000** girls aged 15–19 versus a regional average of roughly 50 — above
   average, not exceptional. The *child marriage / early union* figures are the strong ones;
   the fertility rate is not.
4. **Any "ranked last in the world" claim on PISA.** Published rankings mix subjects and
   subnational entries. "Among the lowest of the 81 education systems tested" is accurate
   and safe.

Broader point: the country's income poverty has been falling fast for two decades — nearly
3 million people moved out of poverty, and GDP per capita grew 4.1% in 2024 against a
regional 1.6%. Overstating material destitution is both inaccurate and unnecessary, because
the learning figures in Section 1 are far more extreme than anything in the poverty data.
